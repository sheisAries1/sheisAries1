Why it matters

Antimicrobial resistance was directly responsible for an estimated 1.14 million deaths in 2021 and associated with 4.71 million. When first-line antibiotics stop working, routine surgery, childbirth and cancer care all become riskier.

Source: GBD 2021 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, The Lancet, 2024.

07 · Know the enemy

The bugs behind the numbers

All three appear on the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (2024). Each is tracked against the antibiotic class that matters most for treating it.

01

Escherichia coli

WHO: Critical vs 3rd-gen cephalosporins

The most common cause of bloodstream and urinary tract infections. Resistance to cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone usually comes from ESBL enzymes that break the drug down, pushing doctors towards last-resort carbapenems.

02

Klebsiella pneumoniae

WHO: Critical vs 3rd-gen cephalosporins

A hospital-associated pathogen behind pneumonia and sepsis, protected by a thick capsule. It is especially good at collecting resistance genes on mobile plasmids and sharing them with other bacteria.

03

Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)

WHO: High vs methicillin

A skin bacterium that turns dangerous once it reaches the blood. Methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA) shrug off the whole penicillin family — yet, where infection control is strong, MRSA rates have fallen sharply.

Read with care

The interactive charts use country data to 2022, the latest year available in machine-readable form for both networks here. The newest reports, WHO GLASS 2025 (2023 data) and ECDC's report for 2024 (published 2025), are quoted as headline figures above. Countries report from different numbers of laboratories and patients, and coverage grows over time, so a rise can partly reflect better testing. Rates from small samples swing year to year. Correlation between use and resistance is not causation. Gaps on the map mean no data was reported, not zero resistance.
